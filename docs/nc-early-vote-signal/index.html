Does NC's early vote predict the final electorate?

A test against North Carolina's general elections from 2012 to 2024, using the state voter file. Everything is measured the same way: Democrats' share of Democratic and Republican ballots, compared with their share of active registrants. Built October 20, 2026, with 2026 returns through October 19.

Bottom line
  • The final electorate sits close to registration every year. At 16, 7, and 1 day out, early ballots ranged from -1.0 to +12.0 points against registration. The final electorate landed between -2.2 and -0.3 every time.
  • The early vote does not predict where in that range it lands. Not for all voters, and not for any voting-history group, at 16 days, 7 days, or the day before. Out of sample, 1 of 15 versions beat simply predicting the average past result, and that one only by a hundredth of a point.
  • 2026 is far outside anything in the history. Early ballots are +21.1 points more Democratic than active registration at 16 days out; the past high at that point was +12.0 (2020). Every history group is above +21, including reliable voters.
  • What history says for 2026: a final electorate about 1.3 points less Democratic than active registration, or Democrats at about 47.6% of major-party voters (90% range 46.1% to 49.1%). Whether an early vote this lopsided breaks that pattern is something no past cycle can tell us.

What we measured

Democratic advantage = Democrats' share of the ballots cast by Democrats and Republicans, minus Democrats' share of active Democratic and Republican registrants. Unaffiliated voters are left out of both sides. It measures party registration, not vote choice. This is the yardstick G. Elliott Morris uses in his North Carolina analysis; our rebuild matches his published 2016 figures (early vote 8.1 points bluer than the rolls at 16 days out, final electorate 1.5 points less blue).

We compute it for early ballots (mail and in-person) at 16 days, 7 days, and 1 day before Election Day, and for every ballot in the final electorate. Voters are also split by whether they voted in the two previous generals: 0 of 2, 1 of 2, or 2 of 2. 2020 is left out of the fits because of pandemic voting; including it does not change any conclusion.

The early vote swings; the final electorate doesn't

Republicans in North Carolina vote later and more often on Election Day, so the early vote nearly always looks bluer than the rolls. By November the gap closes, and the final electorate ends up a point or two less Democratic than registration.

A bluer early vote has not meant a bluer final electorate

Each dot is one election. If the early vote predicted the final electorate, the dots would rise from left to right.

By voting history

The 2026 surge is not coming only from new or irregular voters. Reliable voters (2/2) are +21.6 points bluer than their registration; their past high at this point was +11.1.

The groups end up in different places. Reliable voters finish within a point of their registration every year (-1.4 to +0.3), because nearly all of them vote; whatever their early lean, it only says when they voted. The movement is in the 0/2 and 1/2 groups.

Testing it honestly

With six elections, a line can fit the past and still predict badly. So each election was predicted from the others, and the misses compared with just using the average past result.

One modest signal: timing-adjusted, a day out

Because the reliable voters' early lean is mostly about timing, subtracting it from the 1/2 group's early lean gives a rough measure of enthusiasm among less regular voters. A day before Election Day, it tracks the 1/2 group's own final result (R² 0.55; typical miss ±2.1 vs. ±2.5 for the average). It does not predict the whole electorate, and at 16 days it predicts nothing. We found it by looking, so treat it as a hypothesis.

In 2026 this number is +2.8 at 16 days, against -8.7 to +0.6 in past cycles: less regular voters are running bluer than reliable ones by more than in any cycle since at least 2012. The day-before number is the one to watch.

Method and caveats

Sources: NCSBE absentee and one-stop files 2012 to 2026, the statewide voter history file (2007 to 2016 from an Internet Archive copy of the December 2016 file), and NCSBE Election Day registration snapshots (active status only; 2026 from the current voter file). Party: absentee file for early ballots, history file for the final electorate, snapshot for registrants. History groups use the generals two and four years earlier. Six elections is a small sample; every conclusion here could change with one more.
