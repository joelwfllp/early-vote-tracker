What North Carolina's early vote can tell us about the final electorate

A test against North Carolina general elections from 2004 to 2024, using state voter files. One measure throughout: Democrats' share of the Democratic and Republican ballots, minus their share of Democratic and Republican registrants. Built October 21, 2026, with 2026 returns through October 19.

What we learned
  • The kind of election does most of the work. The final electorate lands within a few points of registration every time. Where it lands depends mostly on the cycle: about 0 in midterms under a Republican president, -0.8 in presidential years, -2.3 in midterms under a Democratic president. Cycle type alone misses by ±0.7; the historical average misses by ±1.1.
  • The early vote as a whole, and among regular voters, tells us little. Across all voters it adds nothing to cycle type. Voters who cast ballots in both of the last two generals finish between -1.4 and +0.3 against their registration every year, whatever their early lean.
  • Irregular voters close to Election Day carry some signal. Among voters who skipped one or both of the last two generals, a bluer early vote a week out and a day out goes with a bluer final electorate in that group (slopes +0.33 to +0.47).
  • So does the youth vote, for young voters. A week out and a day out, voters under 40 whose early ballots run bluer end up in a bluer final electorate for their age group. The 26-to-40 band is the most reliable (slope about +0.25, and it holds without the 2010 wave). No age band improves the forecast for the whole electorate.
  • 2026 is a midterm under a Republican president, and far outside history. Cycle type alone points to a final electorate about 0 against active registration (Democrats about 48.9% of major-party voters; 90% range 47.6% to 50.3%). The early vote is +21.1 at 16 days out, against a past high of +12.0, and every group is similarly lopsided. The signals that work arrive a week out.

How we measure

Democratic advantage = Democrats' share of the Democratic and Republican ballots, minus Democrats' share of Democratic and Republican registrants. Unaffiliated voters are left out of both sides. It measures party registration, not vote choice. We compute it for early ballots (mail and in-person) 16 days, 7 days, and 1 day before Election Day, and for the whole final electorate. The yardstick is G. Elliott Morris's, and our numbers match his published ones.

Every test predicts each election from the others ("typical miss," leave-one-out), and compares that miss with simply using the average past result. 2020 is left out of every fit because of pandemic voting. 2014 is left out at 16 days because in-person early voting began 12 days out that year.

1. The kind of election does most of the work

Midterms under a Republican president (2006, 2018) produced final electorates at or near registration (+0.4 and -0.3). Midterms under a Democratic president (2010, 2014, 2022) produced the three least Democratic. Presidential years fall between.

Cycle type alone cuts the typical miss by a third or more. Adding the overall early vote does not help: it is worse at 16 days and 1 day out and about even a week out. Starting the sample in 2008 makes the early vote look useful, but that period has only one midterm under a Republican president (2018) and leans heavily on the 2010 wave.

2. The early vote as a whole, and among regular voters, tells us little

Republicans in North Carolina vote later and more on Election Day, so the early vote usually looks bluer than the rolls. It has ranged from -6.9 to +8.1 points against registration. The final electorate stays between -3.1 and +0.4.

Splitting voters by whether they voted in the last two generals (0 of 2, 1 of 2, 2 of 2) shows where the movement is, and isn't. Regular voters (2/2) finish between -1.4 and +0.3 every year, because nearly all of them vote; their early lean only says when they voted (slopes -0.01 to +0.10).

3. Irregular voters close to Election Day carry some signal

In the 0/2 and 1/2 groups, a week out and a day out, every slope is positive (+0.33 to +0.47). The 1/2 relationship is tight except for 2022, when the group (mostly people who had voted in 2020 but not 2018) finished far more Republican than its early vote suggested.

A looser split, by whether someone voted in the previous general only, lets 2010 in. Among voters who skipped the last general, the early lean appears to track their final electorate (R² 0.62 to 0.67), but the fit rests on 2010, far out at the lower left. Without it, R² falls to 0.00 to 0.18.

4. So does the youth vote

A week out and a day out, the under-40 bands show the clearest relationship, and the 26-to-40 band is the most reliable. A week out its slope is +0.25 (R² 0.61), and its typical miss is ±1.3 against ±1.8 for its historical average. Without the 2010 Republican wave (lower left in every panel), it is about the same (slope +0.24, R² 0.51). For 18-to-25-year-olds the slope is larger (+0.38) but noisier, and without 2010 the fit is weaker (R² 0.33). Voters 41 and older show nothing once 2010 is set aside, and no band shows a reliable relationship at 16 days.

Why young voters and not old ones: young voters' final lean moves a lot from cycle to cycle (a spread of about 10 points for 18-to-25-year-olds), because whether they turn out depends on the year. Voters over 65 turn out nearly every time, so their final lean barely moves (about 2 points) and their early lean mostly reflects when they vote.

This is young voters predicting young voters. For the whole electorate, no age band's early lean improves on cycle type. By race, White voters' early lean tracks their own final electorate a week out (R² 0.71); Black voters' registration is so heavily Democratic that the measure barely moves.

5. Where 2026 stands

Every group, including regular voters and every age band, is running far bluer against registration than in any past cycle at this point. At 16 days, the only signal with a track record is cycle type, which points to a final electorate about 0 against active registration. The ones to watch next are the 1/2-group and under-40 measures at 7 days out (October 27) and the day before.

Method and caveats

Sources: NCSBE absentee and one-stop files (2004 to 2026); Election Day registration snapshots (2006 to 2024; the 2006 snapshot is from October 20); statewide voter history (individual records from 2007, via the Internet Archive's December 2016 copy); NCSBE aggregate vote and registration counts by party, age, and race. All-voter and history-group measures use active registrants; age and race use active and inactive registrants. History groups use the generals two and four years earlier (0/2, 1/2, 2/2), or two years earlier only. Cycle type: midterm under a Republican president (2006, 2018), midterm under a Democratic president (2010, 2014, 2022), presidential year. Samples are small (6 to 10 elections) and many subgroups were tested, so treat each result as tentative, especially those that rest on one or two elections. The 2010 Republican wave is the most influential single election wherever it is in the sample.
