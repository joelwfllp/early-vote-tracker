What North Carolina's early vote can tell us about the final electorate

A test against North Carolina general elections from 2004 to 2024, using state voter files. One measure throughout: Democrats' share of the Democratic and Republican ballots, minus their share of Democratic and Republican registrants. Built October 21, 2026, with 2026 returns through October 19.

What we learned
  • The kind of election does most of the work. The final electorate lands within a few points of registration every time. Where it lands depends mostly on the cycle: about +0.2 in midterms under a Republican president, -1.0 in presidential years, -2.2 in midterms under a Democratic president. Cycle type alone misses by ±0.65; the historical average misses by ±1.10.
  • The early vote as a whole, and among regular voters, tells us little. Across all voters it adds nothing to cycle type, and neither does adding any one slice of it. Voters who cast ballots in both of the last two generals finish between -1.4 and +0.3 against their registration every year, whatever their early lean.
  • Without cycle type, irregular voters' early lean carries some signal. Among voters who skipped one or both of the last two generals, a bluer early vote a week out and a day out goes with a bluer final electorate in that group (slopes +0.33 to +0.47). Cycle type explains as much of their final lean, or more.
  • Young voters' early lean is the one piece that helps, a week out. Voters under 41 whose early ballots run bluer end up in a bluer final electorate for their age group, beyond what cycle type predicts. Modeling them that way, older voters from cycle type alone, and adding the two back up beats cycle type on the whole electorate a week out (typical miss ±0.51 vs. ±0.65), and that holds when any single election is left out. A day out it is ahead on the full record (±0.54) but less sturdy, and at 12 days it does not hold up.
  • 2026 is a midterm under a Republican president, and far outside history. Cycle type alone points to a final electorate about +0.2 against active registration (Democrats about 49.1% of major-party voters; 90% range 47.9% to 50.4%). The early vote is +15.5 at 12 days out, against a past high of +8.2. That points the same way cycle type does, but past midterms under a Republican president had ordinary early votes (+0.5 in 2006, +3.9 in 2018), so the size of this one is outside anything in the record. The age-split forecast, the one that works a week out, will be the test.
  • Ten elections is a small sample. If Democrats beat the cycle-type expectation on turnout this year, with an early-vote lead this large, that would suggest the early vote carries signal we cannot yet detect.

How we measure

Democratic advantage = Democrats' share of the Democratic and Republican ballots, minus Democrats' share of Democratic and Republican registrants. Unaffiliated voters are left out of both sides. It measures party registration, not vote choice. We compute it for early ballots (mail and in-person) 12 days, 7 days, and 1 day before Election Day, and for the whole final electorate. The yardstick is G. Elliott Morris's, and our numbers match his published ones.

Every test predicts each election from the others ("typical miss," leave-one-out), and compares that miss with simply using the average past result. 2020 is left out of every fit because of pandemic voting. 2014 is left out at cuts more than 12 days out because in-person early voting began 12 days out that year.

1. The kind of election does most of the work

Midterms under a Republican president (2006, 2018) produced final electorates at or near registration (+0.4 and -0.3). Midterms under a Democratic president (2010, 2014, 2022) produced the three least Democratic. Presidential years fall between.

The model treats cycle type as one variable: midterms pull the electorate toward the party out of the White House, by the same amount either way (1.2 points against presidential years). With only two midterms under a Republican president, that is sturdier than estimating each midterm type on its own.

Cycle type alone cuts the typical miss by a third or more. Adding the overall early vote does not help: it is worse at 12 days and 1 day out and about even a week out. Starting the sample in 2008 makes the early vote look useful a week out, but that rests on the 2010 wave: drop 2010 and it is gone; start in 2004 and it shrinks to a few hundredths.

The same is true of every slice of the early vote we tried. Adding any age band, any voting-history group, or voters who did or did not vote in the last general to cycle type, one at a time, never cut the typical miss by more than 0.03 points, and made it worse in 29 of 30 tries. What does help is modeling young and older voters separately (section 4).

2. The early vote as a whole, and among regular voters, tells us little

Republicans in North Carolina vote later and more on Election Day, so the early vote usually looks bluer than the rolls. It has ranged from -4.8 to +8.2 points against registration. The final electorate stays between -3.1 and +0.4.

Splitting voters by whether they voted in the last two generals (0 of 2, 1 of 2, 2 of 2) shows where the movement is, and isn't. Regular voters (2/2) finish between -1.4 and +0.3 every year, because nearly all of them vote; their early lean only says when they voted (slopes +0.07 to +0.10).

3. Irregular voters: signal that cycle type mostly already captures

In the 0/2 and 1/2 groups, a week out and a day out, every slope is positive (+0.33 to +0.47). The 1/2 relationship is tight except for 2022, when the group (mostly people who had voted in 2020 but not 2018) finished far more Republican than its early vote suggested.

Much of this is the cycle showing up in both numbers. For 0/2 voters, cycle type alone predicts their final lean better than their early lean does a week out (typical miss ±2.0 vs. ±2.8). For 1/2 voters, the early lean does a little better alone (±2.6 vs. ±2.9), but combining the two does worse out of sample. Without cycle type, irregular voters' early lean is informative; with it, little reliable signal is left.

A looser split, by whether someone voted in the previous general only, lets 2010 in. Among voters who skipped the last general, the early lean appears to track their final electorate (R² 0.62 to 0.65), but the fit rests on 2010, far out at the lower left. Without it, R² falls to 0.11 to 0.18.

4. Young voters: the one piece that helps, a week out

A week out and a day out, the two youngest bands show the clearest relationship, and the 26-to-40 band is the most reliable. A week out its slope is +0.26 (R² 0.66), and its typical miss is ±1.2 against ±1.8 for its historical average. Without the 2010 Republican wave (lower left in every panel), it is about the same (slope +0.20, R² 0.50). For 18-to-25-year-olds the slope is larger (+0.38) but noisier, and without 2010 the fit is weaker (R² 0.28). Voters 41 and older show nothing once 2010 is set aside, and no band shows a reliable relationship at 12 days.

Why young voters and not old ones: young voters' final lean moves a lot from cycle to cycle (a spread of about 10 points for 18-to-25-year-olds), because whether they turn out depends on the year. Voters over 65 turn out nearly every time, so their final lean barely moves (about 3 points) and their early lean mostly reflects when they vote.

Unlike the history groups, this survives cycle type. Cycle type alone explains little of young voters' final lean (26 to 40, a week out: typical miss ±2.0, R² 0.21); adding their early lean cuts the miss to ±1.1 (R² 0.79).

That signal also improves the forecast for the whole electorate, but only if young and older voters are modeled separately. Predict voters under 41 from their own early lean plus cycle type, voters 41 and older from cycle type alone, weight each by its usual share of the electorate in that kind of year, and add them up:

A week out the split misses by ±0.51 against ±0.65 for cycle type alone, and it wins in every check we ran: leaving out any single election, and weighting by the plain average age mix instead of the cycle-type average (smallest edge 0.05 points, without 2018). A day out it misses by ±0.54 on the full record, but the edge nearly disappears without 2006. At 12 days it does not hold up: it loses without 2018.

Adding young voters' early lean straight to the whole-electorate model does not work; it has to predict only the young voters' part, weighted by their share. Two cautions. This split was found after trying several others, and ten elections is a short record. And using each year's actual age mix does a bit worse (±0.60 a week out) than using the usual mix, so part of the gain comes from smoothing out year-to-year swings in young turnout. Treat it as a forecast to test, not a settled result.

By race, White voters' early lean tracks their own final electorate a week out (R² 0.71); Black voters' registration is so heavily Democratic that the measure barely moves.

5. Where 2026 stands

The early vote points the same way cycle type does: Democrats are running well in a midterm under a Republican president. But in past cycles the early lean barely sorted by cycle type (correlation +0.19), and past midterms like this one had ordinary early votes. 2026's +15.5 is far beyond anything its cycle type has produced.

Every group, including regular voters and every age band, is running far bluer against registration than in any past cycle at this point. At 12 days, the only signal with a track record is cycle type, which points to a final electorate about +0.2 against active registration. The age-split forecast, using returns 12 days out, puts Democrats at 49.4% of major-party voters (+0.5), but it has no track record this early, and under-41 voters' early lean (+29.6) is far beyond anything it was built on, so it is an extrapolation. The real test is the split forecast a week out (October 27) and the day before.

All of this rests on ten elections. If Democrats beat the cycle-type expectation on turnout this year, with an early-vote lead this large, that would suggest the early vote carries signal that the history so far is too short to show.

Method and caveats

Sources: NCSBE absentee and one-stop files (2004 to 2026); Election Day registration snapshots (2006 to 2024; the 2006 snapshot is from October 20); statewide voter history (individual records from 2007, via the Internet Archive's December 2016 copy); NCSBE aggregate vote and registration counts by party, age, and race. All-voter, history-group, and age measures use active registrants (age by band from the same snapshots; November 2005 for 2004); race uses active and inactive registrants. History groups use the generals two and four years earlier (0/2, 1/2, 2/2), or two years earlier only. Cycle type: midterm under a Republican president (2006, 2018), midterm under a Democratic president (2010, 2014, 2022), presidential year. Samples are small (6 to 10 elections) and many subgroups were tested, so treat each result as tentative, especially those that rest on one or two elections. The 2010 Republican wave is the most influential single election wherever it is in the sample.
