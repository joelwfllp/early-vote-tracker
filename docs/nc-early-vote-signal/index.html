Does NC's early vote predict the final electorate?

A test against seven North Carolina general elections, 2012 to 2024, using the state voter file. Built October 20, 2026, with 2026 returns through October 19 (15 days out).

Bottom line
  • A week out, yes. At 7 days and 1 day out, the final electorate's partisan net is roughly 0.7 × the early-voter net, minus 2 points. Typical miss: about 2 to 3 points.
  • 15 days out (now), loosely. The all-voter number misses by about ±3.9 points.
  • The best single signal is voters who cast a ballot in one of the last two generals (the "1/2" group). On its own it beats the all-voter number at every cut, and at 15 days it misses by about ±2.3.
  • 2026 is off the historical chart. Early voters are D+24.0; the highest past cycle at this point was D+21.1 (2012). The models project a final electorate of D+9.2 ± 4.0 (all voters) to D+12.4 ± 3.1 (1/2 group), ± one standard error.

What we measured

Net means registered Democrats minus registered Republicans, as a share of everyone who voted. It is party registration, not vote choice. A D+10 electorate does not mean Democrats win by 10.

Voters are split by whether they voted in the two previous general elections (one presidential, one midterm): 0 of 2, 1 of 2, or 2 of 2. For each past cycle we compare early voters at 15, 7, and 1 day out with the full final electorate.

A week out, early voting tracks the final result closely

Each dot is one election. The further right, the more Democratic the early vote; the higher up, the more Democratic the final electorate. At 7 days and 1 day out, the dots fall near a line. At 15 days out they are looser.

The early vote always looks more Democratic than the final electorate, because Republicans in NC vote later and more on Election Day. The line captures that discount: at 7 days out, final ≈ -2.3 + 0.69 × early.

The "1 of 2" voters carry the signal

When the early vote is split into the three history groups, the 1/2 group does most of the predictive work. That is because it is where the electorate actually changes from one election to the next. Reliable voters (2/2) look about the same every cycle. The 1/2 group swings.

Here is the same scatter as above at 15 days out, but using only the 1/2 group's early vote to predict the whole final electorate. The dots sit closer to the line.

Testing it honestly: predict each election without peeking

With only seven elections, a model can fit the past well and still predict badly. So each model was refit seven times, each time leaving one election out and predicting it. The bars show the typical size of those misses. Shorter is better.

Putting all three groups in one regression overfits: four numbers estimated from six or seven elections. It looks great in-sample (R² above 0.8) but predicts worse than the simple models. The 1/2 group alone does better than all voters combined: ±2.3 vs. ±3.9 points at 15 days, and ±2.1 vs. ±3.1 at 7 days.

What it says about 2026, for now

Three cautions. First, every 2026 number is outside anything in the history, so the line is being extended past its data. The standard errors account for that, which is part of why they are wide. Second, the three-group model's standard error (±4.6) looks reasonable, but its out-of-sample miss is ±27.1; trust the miss, not the error bar. Third, the 1/2 finding was found by looking at all three groups; it is a hypothesis until it holds again.

The real read comes after the October 27 returns (7 days out), when the models are much tighter.

Method and caveats

Sources: NCSBE absentee/early files for 2012 to 2026 and the statewide voter history file; 2007 to 2016 history comes from an Internet Archive copy of the December 2016 file. The 2012 and 2014 absentee files lack voter IDs and were matched through election-day registration snapshots (99.9% matched). 2010 is excluded because its history groups would need the 2006 general. North Carolina's registration has drifted away from Democrats since 2012; early and final nets drift together, which inflates in-sample R². Full regressions: out/retro_regressions.xlsx.
