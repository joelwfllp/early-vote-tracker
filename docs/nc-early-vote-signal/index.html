Does NC's early vote predict the party mix of the final electorate?

A test against seven North Carolina general elections, 2012 to 2024, using the state voter file. Built October 20, 2026, with 2026 returns through October 19 (15 days out).

Bottom line
  • Registration predicts the final electorate better than the early vote does. In every general from 2012 to 2024, the final electorate's net was the registrants' net minus 1.0 to 4.3 points (average 2.6), because Republicans turn out at higher rates. That rule alone misses by about ±1.2 points. The early-vote models miss by ±2.3 to ±3.9 at 15 days and ±2.3 to ±2.4 even 1 day out.
  • The early-vote fits mostly track registration drift. Democrats' registration edge fell from D+12.3 in 2012 to D+1.4 in 2024, and both the early and final nets fell with it. Measured against registration, the early vote explains almost none of the final electorate's turnout gap (R² 0.06 or less).
  • A possible signal a week out, in the 1/2 group, and it is fragile. Measure each party's early turnout rate (early voters per registrant of that party) among voters who cast a ballot in one of the last two generals. Counting all registrants, the Democratic-to-Republican ratio of those rates at 7 days and 1 day out tracks the final D-R turnout gap (R² 0.68 and 0.76). Counting only active registrants, it mostly fades (R² 0.38 and 0.48, barely better than the historical average). At 15 days it predicts nothing either way.
  • G. Elliott Morris's measure, split by voting history, agrees. No history group's early lean predicts the final electorate at any point (1 of 15 versions beat the historical average out of sample). But 2026 is far outside history in every group, including reliable voters, and stays unusual after adjusting for when reliable voters vote.
  • 2026: registration is even, so history points to R+2.5 ± 1.2. But early voters are +24.0 points more Democratic than registrants, nearly double any past cycle (+13.3 in 2020). Nothing in the history says a 15-day gap carries into the final electorate, and nothing in the history looks like this one: the 1/2-group turnout-rate ratio is 2.76× (past high at this point: 1.30× in 2020). The early-vote models, which extend their lines far past the data, say D+9.2 to D+12.4.

What we measured

Net means registered Democrats minus registered Republicans, as a share of everyone who voted. It is party registration, not vote choice or turnout. In 2012, 44.2% of voters were registered Democrats and 32.9% Republicans, a D+11.3 electorate, and Romney still carried the state. Republicans also turned out at a higher rate that year (72.7% vs. 69.9%). The electorate was D+11 because registration was D+12.

Voters are split by whether they voted in the two previous general elections (one presidential, one midterm): 0 of 2, 1 of 2, or 2 of 2. For each past cycle we compare early voters at 15, 7, and 1 day out with the full final electorate.

Registration does most of the work

The registrants' net and the final electorate's net move together. The final electorate is a little more Republican than the registration rolls every time, by 1.0 to 4.3 points.

Take registration out of both sides, so each axis measures how much more Democratic a group is than the registration rolls. The relationship disappears. Cycles where the early vote ran far ahead of registration (2016, 2020) did not end with a more Democratic final electorate.

Without adjusting for registration, early voting appears to track the final result

This is the original analysis. Each dot is one election. The further right, the more Democratic the early vote; the higher up, the more Democratic the final electorate. At 7 days and 1 day out, the dots fall near a line. Most of that line is the registration drift shown above: early and final nets both slide toward Republicans from 2012 to 2024.

The early vote always looks more Democratic than the final electorate, because Republicans in NC vote later and more on Election Day. The line captures that discount: at 7 days out, final ≈ -2.3 + 0.69 × early.

Among early voters, the "1 of 2" group carries the most signal

When the early vote is split into the three history groups, the 1/2 group does most of the predictive work. That is because it is where the electorate actually changes from one election to the next. Reliable voters (2/2) look about the same every cycle. The 1/2 group swings.

Measured against registration within each group, the composition version of this signal mostly goes away too (out/retro_vs_reg_groups_fits.csv). Turnout rates, below, are where it survives.

Here is the same scatter as above at 15 days out, but using only the 1/2 group's early vote to predict the whole final electorate. The dots sit closer to the line.

Turnout rates by history group: the signal that survives

A cleaner question than "how Democratic is the early vote" is "how fast is each party turning out." For each history group, take Democratic early voters as a share of Democratic registrants in that group, and the same for Republicans. The target is the final turnout gap: Democratic turnout rate minus Republican, across all registrants. Republicans out-turned Democrats in every cycle, by 3.0 to 7.3 points.

In the 1/2 group, the ratio of the two early rates tracks that final gap at 7 days and 1 day out. It does not at 15 days. The 0/2 and 2/2 groups and all voters together do worse (out/retro_turnout_rate_fits.csv).

Cautions. The result depends on who counts as registered. Inactive registrants (11 to 17% of the rolls, mostly people who have moved or stopped voting) lean Democratic and rarely vote, and their number swings with list maintenance. Using only active registrants, the 1/2-group fit drops to R² 0.38 at 7 days and 0.48 at 1 day, with misses of ±1.5 and ±1.4 against ±1.6 for the average. This is also seven elections, and the 1/2 group was singled out after looking at all three, so treat it as a hypothesis. And 2026 will likely sit far outside the historical range at 7 days too, so even the 7-day line will be extended past its data.

G. Elliott Morris's measure, by voting history

Morris's North Carolina analysis (Strength In Numbers, October 20) asks the same question with a cleaner yardstick: Democrats' share of the Democratic and Republican ballots, minus their share of active Democratic and Republican registrants. He drops 2020. Our rebuild of his measure matches his published figures (2016: early vote 8.1 points bluer than the rolls at 16 days, final electorate 1.5 points less blue). Here we split it by voting history.

The 2026 surge is not coming from new or irregular voters alone. Reliable voters (2/2) are +21.6 points bluer than their registration; the past high at this point was +11.1.

The final results look nothing like the early ones. The 2/2 group's final advantage was between -1.4 and +0.3 every cycle, because nearly all of them vote. Whatever their early lean, it only says when they voted.

Timing-adjusted. Because the 2/2 group's early lean is mostly timing, subtracting it from the 1/2 group's early lean gives a rough measure of enthusiasm among less regular voters. It is the best result in this set, and it is modest: at 1 day out it tracks the 1/2 group's own final result (R² 0.55; miss ±2.1 vs. ±2.5), but it does not predict the whole electorate, and at 16 days it predicts nothing. We found it by looking, so it is a hypothesis.

In 2026 the timing-adjusted number is +2.8 at 16 days, against -8.7 to +0.6 in past cycles. Less regular voters are running bluer than reliable ones by more than in any cycle since at least 2012. History cannot say what that means for November until the 7-day and 1-day numbers come in.

Testing it honestly: predict each election without peeking

With only seven elections, a model can fit the past well and still predict badly. So each model was refit seven times, each time leaving one election out and predicting it. The bars show the typical size of those misses. Shorter is better.

Putting all three groups in one regression overfits: four numbers estimated from six or seven elections. It looks great in-sample (R² above 0.8) but predicts worse than the simple models. The 1/2 group alone does better than all voters combined: ±2.3 vs. ±3.9 points at 15 days, and ±2.1 vs. ±3.1 at 7 days.

What it says about 2026, for now

The registration-only number is the one the history supports best. The question for 2026 is whether an early vote this far ahead of registration means something new. History can't answer that: it has no cycle like it.

Three cautions about the early-vote models. First, every 2026 number is outside anything in the history, so the line is being extended past its data. The standard errors account for that, which is part of why they are wide. Second, the three-group model's standard error (±4.6) looks reasonable, but its out-of-sample miss is ±27.1; trust the miss, not the error bar. Third, the 1/2 finding was found by looking at all three groups; it is a hypothesis until it holds again.

The October 27 returns (7 days out) will tighten the early-vote models, but in past cycles even the 7-day and 1-day models did not beat registration alone.

Method and caveats

Sources: NCSBE absentee/early files for 2012 to 2026 and the statewide voter history file; 2007 to 2016 history comes from an Internet Archive copy of the December 2016 file. The 2012 and 2014 absentee files lack voter IDs and were matched through election-day registration snapshots (99.9% matched). 2010 is excluded because its history groups would need the 2006 general. Election-day registration by party: NCSBE voter statistics files (active and inactive). North Carolina's registration has drifted away from Democrats since 2012; early and final nets drift together, which inflates in-sample R² in the unadjusted models. Full regressions: out/retro_regressions.xlsx (unadjusted) and out/retro_vs_reg_fits.csv (registration).
